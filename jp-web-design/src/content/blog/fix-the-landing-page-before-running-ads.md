A business owner asks me to run Google Ads. I look at the site the ads would point to, and the honest answer is: not yet. That conversation is awkward, because it sounds like upselling. It is not, and here is the specific reason why.

## Ads buy attention. The page decides what it is worth.

Advertising does one thing: it puts your business in front of someone who was not otherwise going to see it. What happens in the next ten seconds is entirely the page's job.

If the page converts poorly, advertising does not fix that — it multiplies it. You are paying for every visitor either way. A page that loses most of them loses most of what you paid for, and it does so at a steady, predictable rate, every day, for as long as the campaign runs.

The uncomfortable part is that this is invisible without tracking. The campaign reports clicks and impressions, and both look healthy. The money disappears quietly on the other side of the click.

## The cost is ongoing, and the fix is one-time

This is the whole argument.

Improving a landing page is a fixed amount of work. You do it once, and every visitor from then on — paid, organic, referral, the business card you handed someone last week — arrives at the better page.

A weak page, by contrast, charges you per visitor, indefinitely. Every month you run ads to it, you pay the same percentage again.

So the order is not a preference. Fixing the page first means the advertising budget buys more from its first day. Running ads first means buying the lesson repeatedly.

## What "not ready" actually means

I am not talking about a redesign. There is a short list of things that make a page unable to convert paid traffic, and most of them are quick:

**The ad promises something the page does not mention.** Someone searches "emergency roof repair," clicks an ad for emergency roof repair, and lands on a homepage about the company's history. They leave. This is the single most common paid-traffic failure, and it is a structural problem: the campaign needed a page about emergency repair, and one did not exist.

**There is no obvious action.** Paid traffic has less patience than organic traffic — they did not seek you out, they were interrupted or they are comparison-shopping several tabs at once. If the next step is not obvious without scrolling, it is not obvious.

**It is slow on a phone.** Paid clicks are disproportionately mobile. A page that takes several seconds to become usable loses people who already cost you money.

**Nothing on the page answers the objection.** Price range, timeline, service area, whether you handle their specific situation. A visitor who cannot resolve the objection resolves it by leaving.

**Conversion tracking does not exist.** This is the one I would fix even before the others, because without it you cannot tell a campaign that is working from one that is not. You are optimising on clicks, which is optimising on the wrong thing.

## The exception

There is one case where running ads first is right: when you genuinely do not know whether there is demand for what you are selling, and a small, deliberately limited test is the cheapest way to find out.

That is a research budget, not a growth budget. Treat it that way — set an amount you are willing to spend to learn something, run it long enough to mean anything, and read the result honestly. Just do not confuse it with a campaign meant to produce customers.

## What I actually recommend

If the page is in reasonable shape, start the ads and improve the page alongside them — that is how I usually sequence [a Google Ads](/services/google-ads) or [Meta Ads](/services/meta-ads) launch.

If the ad would land somewhere that cannot answer the question it was clicked on, build that page first. It is usually [one page, not a website](/services/web-design-development).

And either way, set up conversion tracking before the first pound of spend. A campaign without it is not a campaign you can learn anything from.
