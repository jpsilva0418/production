Most small-business websites I am asked to look at are not ugly. They are unclear. The design is fine, the photos are fine, and the business is genuinely good at what it does — but a stranger landing on the homepage cannot work out, in the first few seconds, what is being offered, whether it applies to them, and what happens next.

That is a conversion problem wearing design's clothes. Here is the short list, in the order a visitor actually needs it.

## 1. What you do and who for, above the fold

The single most common failure is a homepage headline that could belong to any business in any industry. "Quality you can trust." "Your satisfaction is our priority." "Welcome to our website."

A visitor arrives with one question: *is this for me?* The headline's job is to answer it immediately. "Professional cleaning for the spaces your life depends on" tells me more in eight words than three paragraphs of mission statement. Name the service, and name who it is for.

If you serve a specific area, say so near the top. A roofer in Worcester and a roofer in Phoenix are not competing, but a visitor cannot tell that from a site that never mentions a location.

## 2. One obvious next action

Count the things a visitor can do on your homepage. If the answer is more than two or three, each one is competing with the others.

Pick the action you actually want — call, request a quote, book a consultation — and make it the loudest thing on the page. Repeat it as the visitor scrolls, because people decide at different points. A secondary action is fine; a menu of eight equally weighted options is not.

On mobile, the primary action should be reachable with a thumb without hunting. A persistent call button at the bottom of the screen is not a design compromise. For a service business where most traffic is mobile, it is the single highest-value element on the page.

## 3. Proof that is specific enough to be checkable

Generic trust signals do nothing. "Licensed and insured" is table stakes; everyone says it. What moves someone is specificity: how long you have been doing this, the kind of jobs you take, photographs of work you actually did, the names of the towns you serve.

Real photographs of real jobs beat stock photography every time, even when the stock photo is technically better. A visitor is not grading your photography. They are trying to decide whether you are real.

If you have reviews, the right place for them is your Google Business Profile, where they carry weight because you do not control them. Copying them onto your own site is weaker — and marking them up as review schema on your own pages is permanently ineligible for rich results, because Google excludes reviews about the entity controlling the page.

## 4. Answers to the questions that stop the call

Every business has three or four questions a customer needs answered before they will pick up the phone. Do you handle insurance claims? Do you work on weekends? How long does it take? Do you charge for an estimate?

Most sites leave these to the contact form, which is backwards: the visitor has to make contact in order to find out whether contact is worth making. Answer them on the page. A FAQ section is the least glamorous and most reliably useful thing you can add.

## 5. A site that works on the phone it is being read on

This is less about design than about weight. A gallery of twelve full-resolution photographs will look superb on the laptop it was designed on and stutter badly on a mid-range Android over a weak connection — which is how a large share of local customers actually arrive.

Fast matters here more than clever. Compress the images, size them properly, and be honest about whether an animation is earning its bytes.

## 6. Structure that search engines can follow

Not an SEO campaign — just the groundwork. One clear topic per page, headings that describe the content beneath them, a page for each service you actually want to be found for, and a title and description written for a human.

A single "Services" page listing nine services will rank for none of them. Nine pages, each genuinely about one service, is a different site to a search engine even though it is the same information.

## The honest summary

Clarity, one obvious action, specific proof, answered objections, speed, and structure. That is most of it.

None of this requires a redesign in the dramatic sense. I have seen sites gain more from rewriting a headline and moving a phone number than from a complete visual rebuild — because the problem was never that it looked bad. It was that nobody could tell what it was for.
